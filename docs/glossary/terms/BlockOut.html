Block Out Mesh

Tags: Modeling, Topology, Greybox, High Poly, Low Poly

Definition Overview:

A “Block Out”, also known as a “Proxy Mesh”, is a simple, low‑detail 3D version of an asset created early in production to define its overall size, shape, and proportions.

Instead of focusing on detail, materials, or topology, the goal is to quickly establish scale, silhouette, and spatial relationships. This allows artists and designers to make changes while they are still fast and inexpensive, avoiding costly revisions later in the pipeline.

Block Outs are also used as functional stand‑ins (Proxy Meshes), allowing other departments—such as level design, set dressing, animation, and layout— to begin working with a usable version of the asset without waiting for the final model.

Conceptually, this is similar to the figure drawing technique of “enveloping the form,” where simple shapes are used to define overall proportions before refining detail.

The term may also be used alongside or interchangeably with “Greyboxing”, which refers to blocking out an entire environment, while Block Out / Proxy Mesh can describe a single asset within that space.

Expanded Explanation:

A Block Out (or Proxy Mesh) is a foundational step in the 3D production pipeline for games, film, and animation. It represents the asset as a simplified collection of basic forms that establish proportion, volume, and placement, without concern for final detail or polish.

This stage is critical because it allows teams to answer key questions early: Does the asset feel the right size? Does it fit the environment? Does it support gameplay or staging?

Making these decisions during the block out stage is significantly more efficient, as adjusting scale or proportions later—after modeling, UVs, texturing, rigging, or animation— becomes increasingly time‑consuming and expensive.

The term “Proxy Mesh” highlights the practical role of a block out as a working stand‑in. Even though it is visually simple, it is highly valuable because it enables multiple departments to work in parallel:

  • Level Design: Can place and test layout, collision, and flow
  • Set Dressing: Can build scenes around correctly scaled assets
  • Animation: Can begin timing and interaction tests
  • Lighting/Layout: Can establish composition and staging early

By providing an early, usable version of the asset, block outs prevent pipeline bottlenecks and allow production to move forward before final assets are complete.

From an artistic perspective, block outs closely relate to the drawing concept of “enveloping the form.” In both cases, the artist first captures the largest, simplest shapes to define proportion and structure before refining smaller details. This ensures a strong foundation and reduces the likelihood of major corrections later.

It is also important to understand how this term is used at different scales:

  • Block Out / Proxy Mesh (Asset Level): Refers to a single prop, character, or object
  • Greyboxing (Environment Level): Refers to blocking out an entire level or scene using simple geometry

While these terms are closely related and sometimes used interchangeably, they typically differ in scope—individual asset vs. full environment.

In summary, a Block Out / Proxy Mesh is not just a rough draft—it is a critical production tool that:

  • Defines scale and proportion early
  • Reduces costly revisions later
  • Enables parallel workflows across departments
  • Supports faster iteration and better team communication
